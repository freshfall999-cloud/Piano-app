# Keyfall

A piano practice app that runs in the browser with no build step: open `index.html`.

- **Practice** – notes fall onto the keyboard and the song waits until you play the right key.
- **Play** – the song runs in time; hits are scored (Perfect / Good), with streaks, accuracy and stars.
- **Listen** – the app plays the song for you.
- Right hand, left hand or both (the other hand is played for you), adjustable speed.
- **Sustain pedal** – when a song uses the pedal, Listen mode (and the accompanying hand in the other modes) plays it with the pedal: notes keep sounding after their keys are let go until the pedal lifts. The pedal comes from MIDI files (controller 64), from recordings transcribed with Transkun (which hears the pedal), or, for built-in pieces normally played with pedal (Prelude in C, Für Elise, Canon in D, Brahms' Lullaby, Greensleeves, Amazing Grace), is changed at every bar or beat. A *Ped.* light shows when it's down, sheet music shows *Ped.* marks, and the start card has a *Pedal* setting to turn it off. Recordings without pedal information let each note ring on to the next one instead.
- **View: falling notes or sheet music** – sheet music shows a full, still page of music (grand staff, several lines per page, bar numbers). The note to play lights up and a blue marker shows where you are; in Practice it waits for you, and the page turns by itself when you reach the next line on a new page. Short songs fit on one page, printed as large as the screen allows. The music is written like printed sheet music: a key signature and time signature, notes snapped to a sixteenth-note grid so every bar adds up, eighths and sixteenths joined by beams, ties, rests, accidentals and naturals that follow the usual rules, and pedal changes shown as notches. Very low or very high stretches are written an octave nearer the staff under an *8vb* / *8va* bracket instead of a ladder of ledger lines, and every line on the printed page gets as much room as its notes need. Recordings are written in a simpler, easier-to-read style (no ties, eighth-note grid unless a note is clearly on a sixteenth). Note names can be shown inside the note heads.
- **Printable sheet music** – the *🖨 Printable sheet music* button on the start card turns any song (including MP3s and MIDI files you added) into ordinary black-and-white sheet music on letter-size pages: title, tempo, time signature, grand staff, rests, bar numbers and pedal marks, for the difficulty chosen on the start card. *Download PDF* saves it as a PDF file (made in the browser, nothing is uploaded) and *Print* sends it to a printer. Note names inside the heads are optional. You can type your own title and "By" line (your name, the composer…) at the top of the print screen; it is remembered for that song and used as the PDF file name.
- **Busy recordings cleaned up** – a piano note finder hears every instrument of an orchestra or band as piano. Before a recording is shown, each moment is trimmed to what a pianist would play: per hand at most four keys within an octave (the melody on top of the right hand and the bass at the bottom of the left kept first, then the loudest), faint ghost notes dropped and quick double hits merged. *Extra hard* only adds octaves where a hand has room.
- **Playable by real hands** – every song (built-in, MIDI or MP3, at every difficulty) is checked chord by chord so neither hand has to stretch more than an octave or press more than five keys at once, or leap further than it can in the time. The split between the hands stays as written when it already works; otherwise notes are shared out again, a note out of reach moves by an octave to a hand that can reach it (same harmony), and a held note is let go when its hand has to move on.
- **3D sparkles** – when you hit a note, sparks burst out of the key toward you, a ring of light spreads over the keys and the key glows (drawn in 3D with three.js on a see-through layer, only while something is moving). Turn it off with *3D sparkles when you hit a note* on the home screen; it is off automatically if the device asks for reduced motion, and the flat sparkles are used instead.
- **Name your songs** – the ✎ button on a song you added renames it (its stars move with it).
- **Which note finder made it** – a song made from a recording says so on its card (“Made with Transkun V2 Aug”), on the start card and in the printout’s “By” line. If the chosen finder failed and the standard one stepped in, it names the one that was actually used. (Songs added before this was saved don’t show it.)
- **Plug in a digital piano** – a digital piano or MIDI keyboard connected by USB cable or Bluetooth counts every key exactly, with no microphone (settings: “🎹 Digital piano”). The piano makes its own sound; the app can add its piano sound for a keyboard without speakers, and follows the sustain pedal. 🎤 Listen is switched off when the piano plays, so notes aren't counted twice. This uses Web MIDI, which Chrome, Edge, Firefox and Android have but Safari (iPad, iPhone, Mac) doesn't: on an iPad, open the app in the free *MIDIWeb Browser* app instead. Alternatively, “🎤 Listen through” picks another audio input for 🎤 Listen, such as a piano that sends its sound over USB or an audio interface (Safari may still use the built-in microphone; the “ignore talking” checks are skipped for a cable input).
- **Difficulty: Easy / Medium / Hard / Extra hard / Extra extra hard** for every song, including uploaded recordings – Easy is the melody alone, Medium the melody plus one bass note, Hard every note as written (or as transcribed), Extra hard a fuller version with the melody and bass in octaves (one-hand songs get a bass line added). Extra extra hard goes further, following the song's own chords (the triad that best fits the notes over each bass note): the right hand plays octave chords (under each melody note of a beat or longer that belongs to the chord, the chord note a third or sixth below fills the octave), the left hand never rests (an Alberti pattern of the chord in half beats, or whole beats in fast songs, kept out of the muddy low register), the added notes are played softer so the melody leads, and the timing is much stricter (a hit counts within 0.13 s instead of 0.22 s, a perfect within 0.05 s instead of 0.08 s). It is still kept playable: no hand stretches past an octave or plays more than five keys.
- **Listen (microphone)** – turn on 🎤 Listen and play a real piano; the app hears each note and waits for the right one.
- **Train on my piano** – play each of the 88 keys once; the app learns how every key sounds through this microphone in this room (and the room's background noise), and listens for each key on the bands where it was actually heard. Training is saved in the browser on that device; untrained keys still use the general listener.
- **AI listening** (on by default) – a trained neural network (Spotify's Basic Pitch) runs on the microphone about four times a second in a background worker and decides which keys were struck; notes register about half a second after they're played. The rule-based listener takes over while it loads or if the device can't run it. (`listen-ml.js`, `ml-worker.js`; the WebAssembly back-end of TensorFlow.js in `vendor/`.)
- **Microphone sensitivity** – Low / Normal / High on the song library screen.
- **Ignore talking** (on by default) – speech is rejected because a piano note keeps ringing at a steady pitch after it is struck, while a syllable of speech fades or slides within a fraction of a second. Notes need to ring about 0.2 s to count; turn it off for very short staccato.
- Notes are judged against the room's own background sound in each frequency range, not against fixed loudness levels, so soft playing in a quiet room is heard and fan or traffic rumble doesn't play the song by itself.
- **Note practice** – a note appears on the staff (treble, bass or both, optional ledger lines and sharps/flats) and you find it on the keyboard.
- Input: computer keyboard (Z row = C3 octave, Q row = C4 upward), mouse/touch, or a MIDI keyboard.

The song library screen lists the built-in songs by difficulty and shows your best star rating. **Add your own song** imports a `.mid` file, or a recording (MP3, M4A or WAV) of piano playing: recordings are turned into notes on the device with Spotify's [Basic Pitch](https://github.com/spotify/basic-pitch-ts) model, loaded only the first time it's needed. Every recording can then be played at any of the four difficulties. **MP3 note finder: Best quality** uses Google Magenta's [Onsets and Frames](https://magenta.tensorflow.org/onsets-frames) piano model instead (`oaf.html`, `vendor/magenta/`, Apache 2.0): on our test recordings it found 99% of the notes with almost no extra ones (Basic Pitch: about 93% on the harder ones), but it is slower and downloads 60 MB the first time. Both run in a background worker (`import-worker.js`, `oaf-worker.js`) and go through the song a few seconds at a time, freeing memory as they go, so the screen stays smooth and a long song needs no more memory than a short one (a 5-minute MP3 used 289 MB and froze the page for seconds before; now about 4 MB on the page, no freezes). If an older device can't use the graphics chip from a worker, Onsets and Frames falls back to a hidden frame (`oaf.html`), still a piece at a time. **Note recognition** on the song screen picks the models: *Hearing your piano* is AI (Spotify Basic Pitch plus the app's own checks, recommended) or Simple (pitch detection only: starts instantly, any device); *MP3 → notes* is Piano, best (Google Onsets and Frames, the default), Piano, fast (Spotify Basic Pitch) or Songs with singing and beats (Basic Pitch with looser thresholds, because it hears every instrument while Onsets and Frames only hears piano). On an original test track with singing, fingerpicked guitar, 808 bass and trap drums (known notes), the song setting found 30/32 sung notes, 73/96 guitar notes and 12/16 bass notes; the piano-tuned Fast setting found 10, 36 and 1, and Onsets and Frames 30, 63 and 0. Each choice shows what it's good at. Two more piano models run with ONNX Runtime Web in `onnx-worker.js`: **Transkun** (Yujia Yan and Zhiyao Duan, MIT) and **ByteDance**'s high-resolution piano transcription (Qiuqiang Kong et al., Apache 2.0, weights CC BY 4.0). Both were converted from PyTorch to ONNX (`vendor/models/`, weights stored as 16-bit floats and expanded on load); the code around the networks (16 s / 10 s overlapping pieces, Transkun's 6-window mel spectrum and semi-Markov Viterbi decoding, ByteDance's onset/offset regression post-processing) is a port of the original Python. Checked against the original Python on our hardest test recordings: Transkun found 140/140 notes with no wrong ones (the browser version: identical on hcanon, 58/58), ByteDance 138/140 with 46 octave echoes (the browser version matches 71 of its 72 notes on hcanon). They use the graphics chip through WebGPU where the browser has it, otherwise the processor.

Also converted the same way: Sony's **hFT-Transformer** (Toyama et al., ISMIR 2023; code MIT, weights CC BY-NC-SA 4.0, so non-commercial use only) and the **Queen Mary University of London / Yamaha** re-training of ByteDance's network for other pianos and rooms (Edwards, Dixon, Benetos, Maezawa and Kusaka, IEEE SPL 2024; CC BY 4.0). On all 20 of our test recordings (1242 notes, original Python versions; the browser versions match on the recordings we compared):

| Model | Notes found | Wrong notes |
|---|---|---|
| Transkun | 99.4% | 0 |
| Queen Mary / Yamaha | 99.6% | 41 |
| Sony hFT-Transformer | 99.5% | 265 |
| ByteDance | 99.4% | 289 |
| Onsets and Frames (9 of them) | 98.8% | 2 |
| Basic Pitch, piano setting | 91% | 29 |

Our test recordings are synthesized pianos, whose strong overtones seem to cause the octave echoes counted as wrong notes for ByteDance and Sony. On a real 4-minute hip-hop song with singing (no note list, so scored by how well the notes' pitch classes follow the recording's chroma, 10 times a second), the song setting matched best (0.52, against 0.30 for the same notes moved a semitone) and covered 86% of the song; the piano models scored 0.42–0.45 and covered 51–74%: they are for solo piano. When a recording is played back, each note rings on to the next note in the same hand (up to 2 beats), as the pedal would, instead of stopping where the finder heard it fade, and any silence longer than 4 beats is shortened to 2 beats. Hands are split at the widest gap in each chord, following the music rather than a fixed middle C. Imported songs are kept in the browser on that device.

**Songs → piano version** (`arrange.js`): for full songs (singing, guitar, drums, 808s), writing down every sound gives a jumble of short notes (on a 4-minute hip-hop song, a quarter of them under 0.15 s, none on the beat). This setting makes a piano arrangement instead, as "song to piano" apps do: Basic Pitch (song thresholds) hears the pitches; a beat tracker (Ellis-style dynamic programming on spectral flux, with a tempo estimate that checks the beat against the kick drum and bass, the bar and the sixteenth-note hi-hats, because trap rhythms alone suggest 4/3 of the tempo) finds the beat; chords (12 major + 12 minor, scored per beat from the pitch activations and the bass, smoothed with a Viterbi pass) give the left hand a bass note and chord at each change and every bar; and a dynamic-programming pass picks one melody line, preferring long, loud, held notes and small steps, for the right hand. Everything is snapped to an even beat grid with bar lines where chords change and the kick lands. On our test track with known notes it found the tempo (84 BPM), the chord of every bar, and 25 of the 32 sung notes; on a real 84 BPM hip-hop song it found 84 BPM and a right-hand line of about one note per beat.

**MuScriptor** (Kyutai and Mirelo, 2026) is the most accurate model for full songs. Its weights (CC BY-NC 4.0) are gated, so this public app never contains them: in the MP3 list, *MuScriptor* asks for the person's own Hugging Face read token (kept in the browser), downloads the small model (103M parameters, 412 MB) straight from huggingface.co once and keeps it in the browser's cache, and runs it on the device (`musc-worker.js`, ONNX Runtime Web, WebGPU when available, with the key/value cache kept on the graphics chip). The ONNX graph (`vendor/models/musc_small.onnx`) holds no weights; each weight is handed over by name from the downloaded safetensors file. The inference loop (5 s pieces, log-mel prefix, greedy decoding with the tie prologue teacher-forced from the previous piece) and the MT3 token decoder are ports of MuScriptor's own code; with a random stand-in model they produce exactly the same 6003-token stream as the original Python. The result goes through the band-to-piano step below. It is slow on a processor (about 0.24 s per token on our test machine) and needs WebGPU to be practical. Kyutai's free website (muscriptor.kyutai.org) is linked as the faster alternative: its MIDI download is then added with "Add your own song". Multi-instrument MIDI files (two or more non-piano tracks) become a two-hand piano part (`KeyfallArrange.fromTracks`): the singer or lead track in the right hand, octave-shifted to fit; the bass and chords of the other tracks (24 triads per beat on the file's own beat grid, Viterbi-smoothed) in the left. Checked on a 5-track MIDI of our test song: all 32 sung notes, the right chord in all 12 bars, tempo exact.

**Transkun V2 Aug** (the augmentation-trained V2 checkpoint from Transkun's model card) is in the list too, for piano recorded on a phone or in a room; it matches the original Python on our canon recording (57/58, no wrong notes). **Demucs + ByteDance**: Meta's Demucs v4 `htdemucs_6s` separates the song into six stems, and ByteDance's model transcribes the piano stem (`onnx-worker.js`, the network converted without its STFT/iSTFT, which are ported, as is Demucs' segment blending; the browser's piano stem matches the original to 54 dB). On our synthetic test mixes it did not beat ByteDance alone (piano + bass + drums: 47/48 notes either way; piano + synthetic singing and plucked guitar: 45 vs 46 notes found, 23 vs 7 wrong, as some of the plucked guitar leaked into the piano stem); it is meant for real songs with a piano part.

**YourMT3+** (Chang, Benetos, Kirchhoff and Dixon, MLSP 2024; Apache-2.0 weights, no account needed) transcribes every instrument: `ymt3-worker.js` runs the unofficial ONNX export of its YPTF.MoE+Multi (noPS) checkpoint (16-bit weights, 95 MB; 244/247 token rows identical to full precision on our test song), with that export's greedy decoding loop and a port of the authors' token → note code. Checked against the original Python on our test song: identical token rows (247/247) and notes (116/116). On that song it was very precise (4 wrong pitched notes) but found 11/32 synthetic sung notes, 64/96 guitar and 2/16 808 bass. Its notes go through the band-to-piano step, which now ignores near-empty lead tracks and falls back to the busiest instrument when nobody sings.

**From a band to piano keys** (`KeyfallArrange.pianoReduce`, used for YourMT3+, MuScriptor and multi-instrument MIDI files): every instrument except drums is kept and moved onto the piano. The singer (or lead) becomes the right hand's top line, with slides and wobbles merged; the bass becomes the left hand's bottom line between E1 and E3 (where no bass was heard, the root of the chord found from all instruments); guitar, keys, strings and pads are moved by octaves to sit under the melody or over the bass. Unisons become one key, each hand plays at most 4 keys at a time within about an octave and a third, and everything is on a sixteenth-note grid of the song's beat. On our 5-track test MIDI: all 32 sung notes on top, 90/96 guitar notes kept, all 16 bass notes at the bottom, never more than 4 keys or wider than a hand.

When releasing an update, run `./bump-version.sh` so browsers fetch the new code instead of a cached copy.

Built-in songs live in `songs.js` as simple note strings and are all public-domain melodies.
