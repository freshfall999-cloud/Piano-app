# Keyfall

A piano practice app that runs in the browser with no build step: open `index.html`.

- **Practice** – notes fall onto the keyboard and the song waits until you play the right key.
- **Play** – the song runs in time; hits are scored (Perfect / Good), with streaks, accuracy and stars.
- **Listen** – the app plays the song for you.
- Right hand, left hand or both (the other hand is played for you), adjustable speed.
- **View: falling notes or sheet music** – sheet music shows a full, still page of music (grand staff, several lines per page, bar numbers). The note to play lights up and a blue marker shows where you are; in Practice it waits for you, and the page turns by itself when you reach the next line on a new page. Short songs fit on one page, printed as large as the screen allows. Note names can be shown inside the note heads.
- **Difficulty: Easy / Medium / Hard / Extra hard** for every song, including uploaded recordings – Easy is the melody alone, Medium the melody plus one bass note, Hard every note as written (or as transcribed), Extra hard a fuller version with the melody and bass in octaves (one-hand songs get a bass line added).
- **Listen (microphone)** – turn on 🎤 Listen and play a real piano; the app hears each note and waits for the right one.
- **Train on my piano** – play each of the 88 keys once; the app learns how every key sounds through this microphone in this room (and the room's background noise), and listens for each key on the bands where it was actually heard. Training is saved in the browser on that device; untrained keys still use the general listener.
- **AI listening** (on by default) – a trained neural network (Spotify's Basic Pitch) runs on the microphone about four times a second in a background worker and decides which keys were struck; notes register about half a second after they're played. The rule-based listener takes over while it loads or if the device can't run it. (`listen-ml.js`, `ml-worker.js`; the WebAssembly back-end of TensorFlow.js in `vendor/`.)
- **Microphone sensitivity** – Low / Normal / High on the song library screen.
- **Ignore talking** (on by default) – speech is rejected because a piano note keeps ringing at a steady pitch after it is struck, while a syllable of speech fades or slides within a fraction of a second. Notes need to ring about 0.2 s to count; turn it off for very short staccato.
- Notes are judged against the room's own background sound in each frequency range, not against fixed loudness levels, so soft playing in a quiet room is heard and fan or traffic rumble doesn't play the song by itself.
- **Note practice** – a note appears on the staff (treble, bass or both, optional ledger lines and sharps/flats) and you find it on the keyboard.
- Input: computer keyboard (Z row = C3 octave, Q row = C4 upward), mouse/touch, or a MIDI keyboard.

The song library screen lists the built-in songs by difficulty and shows your best star rating. **Add your own song** imports a `.mid` file, or a recording (MP3, M4A or WAV) of piano playing: recordings are turned into notes on the device with Spotify's [Basic Pitch](https://github.com/spotify/basic-pitch-ts) model, loaded only the first time it's needed. Every recording can then be played at any of the four difficulties. **MP3 note finder: Best quality** uses Google Magenta's [Onsets and Frames](https://magenta.tensorflow.org/onsets-frames) piano model instead (`oaf.html`, `vendor/magenta/`, Apache 2.0): on our test recordings it found 99% of the notes with almost no extra ones (Basic Pitch: about 93% on the harder ones), but it is slower and downloads 60 MB the first time. Both run in a background worker (`import-worker.js`, `oaf-worker.js`) and go through the song a few seconds at a time, freeing memory as they go, so the screen stays smooth and a long song needs no more memory than a short one (a 5-minute MP3 used 289 MB and froze the page for seconds before; now about 4 MB on the page, no freezes). If an older device can't use the graphics chip from a worker, Onsets and Frames falls back to a hidden frame (`oaf.html`), still a piece at a time. **Note recognition** on the song screen picks the models: *Hearing your piano* is AI (Spotify Basic Pitch plus the app's own checks, recommended) or Simple (pitch detection only: starts instantly, any device); *MP3 → notes* is Piano, best (Google Onsets and Frames, the default), Piano, fast (Spotify Basic Pitch) or Songs with singing and beats (Basic Pitch with looser thresholds, because it hears every instrument while Onsets and Frames only hears piano). On an original test track with singing, fingerpicked guitar, 808 bass and trap drums (known notes), the song setting found 30/32 sung notes, 73/96 guitar notes and 12/16 bass notes; the piano-tuned Fast setting found 10, 36 and 1, and Onsets and Frames 30, 63 and 0. Each choice shows what it's good at. Two more piano models run with ONNX Runtime Web in `onnx-worker.js`: **Transkun** (Yujia Yan and Zhiyao Duan, MIT) and **ByteDance**'s high-resolution piano transcription (Qiuqiang Kong et al., Apache 2.0, weights CC BY 4.0). Both were converted from PyTorch to ONNX (`vendor/models/`, weights stored as 16-bit floats and expanded on load); the code around the networks (16 s / 10 s overlapping pieces, Transkun's 6-window mel spectrum and semi-Markov Viterbi decoding, ByteDance's onset/offset regression post-processing) is a port of the original Python. Checked against the original Python on our hardest test recordings: Transkun found 140/140 notes with no wrong ones (the browser version: identical on hcanon, 58/58), ByteDance 138/140 with 46 octave echoes (the browser version matches 71 of its 72 notes on hcanon). They use the graphics chip through WebGPU where the browser has it, otherwise the processor. When a recording is played back, each note rings on to the next note in the same hand (up to 2 beats), as the pedal would, instead of stopping where the finder heard it fade, and any silence longer than 4 beats is shortened to 2 beats. Hands are split at the widest gap in each chord, following the music rather than a fixed middle C. Imported songs are kept in the browser on that device.

Third-party files: piano samples in `samples/` (Salamander Grand Piano, CC BY 3.0, see `samples/CREDITS.md`); `vendor/basic-pitch/` (Basic Pitch model and code, Apache 2.0) and `vendor/tf.min.js` (TensorFlow.js 3.21, Apache 2.0).

When releasing an update, run `./bump-version.sh` so browsers fetch the new code instead of a cached copy.

Built-in songs live in `songs.js` as simple note strings and are all public-domain melodies.
